Mapping healthcare access gaps in Nairobi County

A facility-level analysis of Nairobi's health infrastructure against Universal Health Coverage and SDG 3 targets — identifying where the county is underserved and what it would take to close the gap.

RoleIndependent analysis
ToolsPython, Jupyter, pandas, matplotlib
Data942 facility records, 2019 census

Context

SDG Target 3.8 calls for universal access to quality healthcare. I wanted to know how close Nairobi County actually is to that — not at the county level, where averages hide a lot, but region by region.

I combined the county's health facility registry with 2019 census sub-county population data, mapped every facility to one of ten regions, and benchmarked each region against WHO and county targets for access, beds, and service availability.

Where the gaps are

Facility count alone doesn't tell you much — a region can look "covered" and still be underserved once you account for population. Once I normalised by population, three regions stood out as the most underserved.

  • Mathare, Kasarani, and Embakasi all sit under 2 facilities per 10,000 people, the lowest density in the county.
  • Dagoretti, Makadara, Embakasi, and Kasarani fall short of the recommended 18 hospital beds per 10,000 people.
  • Mathare has zero cots anywhere in the region — a specific, fixable gap in neonatal and paediatric care.

Recommendations

  • Quick winReopen the 19 closed facilities before building anything new — especially the one already equipped with 50 beds and 20 cots that operates 24 hours.
  • Primary careExpedite the 6 facilities pending opening to strengthen outpatient access immediately.
  • KasaraniPriority region for new investment — it's short on facilities, beds, family planning, home-based care, and childhood illness services simultaneously.
  • MathareSmallest population of the underserved regions but the only one with zero cots — a targeted, low-cost fix rather than a large capital project.

What I'd do next

If I extended this, I'd want to weight recommendations by estimated cost per facility/bed rather than treating every gap as equally urgent — reopening an existing facility is usually more efficient than greenfield expansion, and it reduces delays in service delivery.
